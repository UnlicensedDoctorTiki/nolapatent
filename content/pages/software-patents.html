Understanding what the software actually does

A product description explains what software does for a customer. A patent application also needs to explain the invention behind that result. A useful technical discussion may involve how components communicate, where processing occurs, how data is represented, or which steps in an algorithm change the way the system operates.

Practical engineering experience helps an attorney ask questions that go beyond a feature list:

  • What technical problem does the invention address, and how did earlier approaches handle it?
  • Which architecture, data structures, or processing steps produce the asserted improvement?
  • Which implementation details are essential, and which could be changed while preserving the approach?
  • How do the components interact, including across devices, servers, or services?

Working through these questions can help distinguish the invention from the surrounding product and identify details that belong in the application. It also gives the inventor an opportunity to correct assumptions before those assumptions become part of the patent disclosure.

Software patent eligibility under 35 U.S.C. § 101

For a developer-focused explanation of eligibility, technical disclosure, and preparing for counsel, read Can Software Be Patented? A Software Patent Attorney Explains.

35 U.S.C. § 101 addresses patent-eligible subject matter. Software-related claims can raise questions about the judicial exception for abstract ideas. Under the Alice/Mayo framework, the analysis considers whether a claim is directed to an exception and, if so, whether its additional elements, individually or in combination, supply an inventive concept.

The USPTO’s examination guidance includes considering whether a claim integrates an exception into a practical application. A claimed improvement to computer functionality or another technology can matter; simply asking a generic computer to perform an abstract task is not enough. Careful drafting should explain the actual improvement and reflect it in the claims, rather than rely on technical labels.

Eligibility depends on the particular claims and disclosure. It is also distinct from novelty, nonobviousness, and the disclosure requirements. Technical detail helps make the invention understandable, but it does not guarantee patent eligibility or issuance.

Drafting the description and claims together

The specification and drawings should explain how the invention works, with terminology the inventors can verify. Depending on the invention, that may call for architecture diagrams, process flows, data structures, pseudocode, or examples of alternative implementations. A statement that a system achieves a desired result may leave unanswered how it achieves that result.

The written-description and enablement requirements of 35 U.S.C. § 112(a) make this more than a matter of style. The application must adequately describe the claimed invention and enable a person skilled in the field to make and use it without undue experimentation.

Patent claims must identify the technical features for which protection is sought, while the specification must adequately support those claims.
