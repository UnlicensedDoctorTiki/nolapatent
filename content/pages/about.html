A career in technology and law

Before becoming an attorney, I studied computer science and psychology at Duke and worked as a software engineer at Motorola, Blastro, and Model Software. That work ranged from Java virtual machine performance to streaming-media applications, databases, and video surveillance. I’ve written code and HTML in Vim, worked with Linux systems and open-source software, and built applications that people used in their work.

At Tulane Law School, my interests in technology and law came together. I wrote about copyright and the First Amendment and worked on software that evaluated copyright rules. Those interests shaped the intellectual property practice I would build after law school.

Today, that practice includes patent preparation and prosecution, patent litigation and PTAB proceedings, trademark protection and enforcement, copyright, trade secrets, and technology agreements. My patent work spans software, mechanical systems, and oil-and-gas technologies. Representative matters give a more concrete picture of that work.

When a matter involves software, I can discuss architecture, algorithms, and implementation with the people who built it. That helps me work through the details of a software patent application, the scope of a development or licensing agreement, or the use of open-source components. For trademarks and copyrights, the same starting point matters: understanding the work, its ownership, and the client’s plans for it.

I’ve also been through the patent process as an inventor. I’m a co-inventor on three issued U.S. patents titled Video surveillance system, involving image processing and object tracking: 7,859,564, 7,956,889, and 8,605,155. Those are distinct from the patents I’ve prosecuted as an attorney.

The Durationator

At Tulane, I developed a general-purpose language, parser, and logic interpreter for the Durationator, translating complicated international copyright rules into executable software.

Professor Elizabeth Townsend Gard’s 2011 article discusses the challenges of developing the tool. Justice Breyer cited pages 216–220 of her article in Golan v. Holder, 565 U.S. 302, 357 (2012) (Breyer, J., dissenting), when discussing the difficulty of determining copyright restoration under 17 U.S.C. § 104A.

Elizabeth Townsend Gard, In the Trenches with § 104A: An Evaluation of the Parties’ Arguments in Golan v. Holder as It Heads to the Supreme Court, 64 Vand. L. Rev. En Banc 199 (2011).

In 2009, I developed and released the first Louisiana Civil Code app for iPhone, along with an app for the Code of Civil Procedure. They made searchable legal references available on a phone. The original iPhone J.D. review and software project page describe that work.

From 2018 to 2021, I served as an Adjunct Professor at Tulane Law School, running the trademark section of its Intellectual Property Law Clinic. I taught students trademark law and supervised their work helping people apply for and obtain trademark registrations.
