Background

From software
to intellectual property.

At Duke, I studied computer science and psychology. The work that followed was practical: Java virtual machine performance at Motorola, streaming media at Blastro, and video-surveillance and business software at Model Software. Writing code meant working through architecture, databases, interfaces, and the details that determine whether a system does what its designer intended.

Tulane Law School gave me another set of systems to understand. My writing examined copyright and the First Amendment; my work on the Durationator put international copyright rules into executable form. Becoming a lawyer brought those interests together: understanding how something works, asking careful questions, and finding a useful way forward.

The Durationator · Tulane Law School

Turning legal rules
into software.

Copyright law, expressed as executable logic.

At Tulane, I developed a general-purpose language, parser, and logic interpreter for the Durationator, software for analyzing copyright terms worldwide. The project required translating complicated legal rules into something a computer could evaluate.

Professor Elizabeth Townsend Gard discussed the challenges of developing the Durationator in her 2011 law review article. Justice Breyer cited pages 216–220 of her article in Golan v. Holder, 565 U.S. 302, 357 (2012) (Breyer, J., dissenting), when discussing the difficulty of determining copyright restoration under 17 U.S.C. § 104A.

Elizabeth Townsend Gard, In the Trenches with § 104A: An Evaluation of the Parties’ Arguments in Golan v. Holder as It Heads to the Supreme Court, 64 Vand. L. Rev. En Banc 199 (2011).
